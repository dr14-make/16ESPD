Derivative and noise

The derivative does not care how big the noise is, only how fast.

Every sensor is noisy

Thermal, shot, flicker, coupling from everything else in the vehicle — noise is not a defect, it is a property. A speedometer has it too.

Why small noise is not safe

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[A\sin(\omega t)\bigr] \;=\; A\,\omega\cos(\omega t) $$

  • Same amplitude A.
  • Frequency ten times higher.
  • Slope ten times steeper.

A derivative reports slope. It does not know or care that the wiggle is small — it only sees how fast it moves.

Above ω = 1 rad/s the derivative amplifies. High frequencies are exactly where sensor noise lives.

Draw the tangents with your finger on the projected plot. This is a visual argument and it works far better as a gesture than as an equation.

Say: any signal is a sum of sine waves — that is what a Fourier transform is telling you. So this is not a special case about sines; it is what a derivative does to every high-frequency component in anything you feed it.

Ask the room: our noise is a hundredth of a km/h. If it sits at 500 rad/s, what does the derivative make of it? (Five km/h per second of apparent acceleration, out of nothing. Now multiply by Td.)

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • A — the amplitude of the noise. Unchanged between the two sines.
  • ω — the angular frequency, radians per second. Ten times higher in the second.
  • A·ω — the amplitude of the derivative. This is the whole argument: differentiation multiplies by frequency.
  • ω = 1 rad/s — the break-even point. Above it the derivative amplifies; below it, attenuates.
  • The yellow tangents — the slope at the steepest point of each sine, which is what the derivative reports.

Say: and now think about what this is physically. That command goes to an engine. Fuelling swinging like that, many times a second, forever. It will not break today — it will wear something out, or overheat something, or simply feel terrible to sit in.

Say: and here is the same lesson as section 08 arriving from a different direction — always plot the actuator command. The controlled variable looked fine in both cases.

Ask the room: would a real cruise control ever run a pure derivative? (No. Which is why the block you get in any real toolchain has a filter coefficient on it, and why most industrial loops are PI with no derivative at all.)

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • Nd = 1000 — filter cutoff pushed so high it is effectively no filter.
  • extrema(...) — the minimum and maximum of the commanded torque, i.e. the full swing.
  • TORQUE_CMD — the signal path for what the controller asks of the engine.
  • delivered = nothing — the delivered trace is suppressed so the commanded chatter is not hidden behind it.

Filter it

A first-order low-pass on the derivative path, with cutoff N in rad/s:

$$ \frac{s\,N}{s+N} \qquad\text{instead of}\qquad s $$

sw = sweep(cruise_noisy(k = 56.0, Ti = 8.0, Td = 1.5),
           "Nd", [1000.0, 100.0, 20.0, 5.0];
           tspan = (0.0, 120.0))

plot_sweep(sw; sig = TORQUE_CMD, name = "Nd")

Say: this is what the Nd parameter is for. Every PID block in every toolchain has one, and most people never touch it because they never look at the actuator command. Now you know what it does and why it is there.

Say: and notice the filter does not remove the noise. It attenuates it, so that after the derivative amplifies what is left, the result is small enough not to matter. Nothing here is exact.

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • sN/(s + N) — a derivative in series with a first-order low-pass. At low frequency it is a derivative; at high frequency it flattens off instead of growing.
  • N — the cutoff frequency in rad/s; Nd in the code.
  • s — the Laplace variable; multiplying by s is differentiating.
  • Nd = 1000, 100, 20, 5 — the sweep, from no filter to heavy.

Choosing the cutoff

Low enough to remove the noise.

High enough to leave the signal.

If those two ranges overlap, no first-order filter will save you and you need a different measurement — a better sensor, or a state estimator that uses a model to tell signal from noise.

Not a recipe There is no formula here, because the answer depends on where your plant's dynamics sit and where your sensor's noise sits. Find both, then choose. Anyone who gives you a single number has assumed your plant.

Say: and the useful case is the common one — the signal you care about is slow and has most of the power, the noise is fast and has very little. When those are well separated, a first-order filter is all you need and the choice is easy.

Say: when they are not separated, that is a Kalman filter conversation, and it is a different course.

Optional aside, only if time and appetite: a filtered derivative sN/(s+N) is algebraically identical to a feedback loop with N forward and an integrator in the feedback path. Same behavior, fewer operations, much harder to read. Worth mentioning that the block in your toolchain may well be implemented the second way.

Terms, if anyone asks

  • Cutoff frequency — where the filter starts attenuating, conventionally at −3 dB.
  • Low-pass — passes slow things, blocks fast ones.
  • The trade-off — too high and the noise gets through; too low and you filter away the signal, and the derivative stops doing its job.
  • When the bands overlap — no first-order filter helps, and you need a better sensor or a state estimator.

What this bought us

  • The derivative's real cost: it amplifies by frequency, and noise is all frequency.
  • A concrete reason the Nd parameter exists.
  • The rule, arriving for the second time today: plot the actuator command.
