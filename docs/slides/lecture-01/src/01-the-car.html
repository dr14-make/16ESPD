What a system is

  • Control inputs u — what we deliberately move.
  • Disturbances d — what moves it anyway.
  • State x — what it remembers.
  • Output y — what we can measure.

Terms, if anyone asks

  • Input — anything that acts on the system. Split into what we choose (u) and what we do not (d).
  • Output — what we can measure. Not the same as the state: a state we cannot observe is not an output.
  • State — the smallest set of numbers that, with the future inputs, determines the future. Loosely: what the system remembers.
  • Disturbance — an input we cannot command. The split between input and disturbance is a modelling choice, not a property of the car.

The force balance

$$ m\,\frac{dv}{dt} \;=\; \underbrace{T\frac{i}{r}}_{\text{tractive}} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,C_dA\,v^2}_{O_V\ \text{drag}} \;-\; \underbrace{f_r\,m\,g\cos\alpha}_{O_f\ \text{rolling}} \;-\; \underbrace{m\,g\sin\alpha}_{O_s\ \text{slope}} $$

Acceleration resistance Oz is the left-hand side: the mass itself. Auxiliary loads Oaux are not modeled here.

Where the torque comes from T is not the commanded torque. Between command and shaft sit a transport delay and a first-order lag — the next slides are about why they are not optional.
Open in Dyad dyad/Vehicle/VehicleBody.dyad

Say: this is first-principles modeling — the white-box method. We are not fitting anything to data; we are writing down the physics we already believe, and then we will be able to argue about every term in it.

Point at: the drag term, v². That square is why this car is nearly linear around 100 km/h and not at all linear between 0 and 100.

They get wrong: the cosine on the rolling term. On a 10 % grade cos α = 0.995, so it looks ignorable — and it is, here. Say that you are keeping it because dropping terms is a decision, not an accident.

Tie back: slide 16 of the existing engine deck names these OV, Of, Oz, Os. Same equation, their notation.

In the model: four things pulling on one flange: the mass, the quadratic drag, the rolling resistance, the grade force. The equation above, as a diagram.

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • m dv/dt — mass times acceleration, N. The left-hand side is Newton's second law along the direction of travel.
  • T·i/r — tractive force, N. T engine torque (N·m), i gear ratio 4.0, r wheel radius 0.31 m.
  • ½ρCdAv² — drag, N. ρ air density, CdA drag area, v speed in m/s.
  • f_r·m·g·cos α — rolling resistance, N. The cosine is 0.995 even on a 10 % slope, so it barely moves.
  • m·g·sin α — the slope term, N.
  • α — road angle. Gradients are given as tan α: 10 % means 0.10.
  • O_V, O_f, O_z, O_s — the course slides' names for drag, rolling, acceleration and slope resistance. Same four terms, their notation.

Terminal speed, on paper first

notebooks/lecture01/01-the-car.ipynb cell 2. The force balance → Terminal speed 
c_drag = 0.5 * CAR.rho * CAR.CdA          # N per (m/s)^2
F_roll = CAR.f_r * CAR.m * CAR.g          # N, flat road
F_max  = CAR.T_max * CAR.i / CAR.r        # N, full torque through the driveline

v_terminal = sqrt((F_max - F_roll) / c_drag)          # m/s

Say: notice what we just did. We set the derivative to zero and solved. That is the steady-state model, and in two notebooks' time an integrator will find this same answer by itself, without being told any of this.

Ask the room, before you show the next slide: 246 km/h from a 150 N·m engine. Does anyone believe that? (Let the discomfort sit for a second — the next slide is the answer, and it lands much harder if they noticed first.)

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • c_drag = ½ρCdA = 0.378 N per (m/s)² — the whole drag law compressed to one coefficient.
  • F_roll = f_r·m·g = 165 N — rolling resistance on the flat.
  • F_max = Tmax·i/r = 1935 N — every newton the engine can put on the road.
  • v_terminal = √((F_max − F_roll)/c_drag) = 68.4 m/s = 246 km/h — where drag eats everything that is left.
  • dv/dt = 0 — the steady-state condition. Setting the derivative to zero is what turns a differential equation into arithmetic.

246 km/h is wrong, and it is wrong on purpose

The model's honest limitation Our engine delivers peak torque at every speed. A real one does not: peak torque lives in a band of a few thousand rpm and falls away on both sides.
  • Every demo in this lecture runs between 90 and 130 km/h, and never asks for more than about 51 N·m of the 150 available. Unaffected.
  • The top end is fiction. Say so now rather than let a student find it later and distrust the parts that are true.
  • Fixing it means a torque map — which is where lecture 2 starts.
Open in Dyad dyad/Vehicle/IdealEngine.dyad

The red curve is the shape of a petrol engine, drawn to make the comparison — not a measurement, and not this car. The blue line is exact: it is what IdealEngine actually computes.

Say: a model is a set of assumptions with a speed limit on it, and the engineering skill is knowing where that limit is. Ours is honest from 0 to about 130 km/h and a lie above it.

Point at the plot, in this order:

  • The blue line. Flat, at 150, all the way across. That is not a simplification of our model — it is our model. IdealEngine contains a limiter whose ceiling is a constant, and nothing in it knows how fast the crankshaft is turning.
  • The red curve. Same peak, but it only reaches it in a band, and it falls away either side. Nothing here is measured — say that out loud — it is the shape every petrol engine has, and the shape is the whole argument.
  • The shaded band. Everything we do today happens in there, roughly 3100 to 4400 rpm, and in there the two curves agree. Our model is fine because of where we are using it, not because it is right.
  • The mark at 8400 rpm. That is where 246 km/h puts the crankshaft. Our model still says 150 N·m. A real engine is down around 95 and falling — so the car never gets there, and the terminal speed we computed is fiction.

Read out: 90 km/h is 3080 rpm, 130 km/h is 4450, and 246 km/h would be 8430. Those come from the same three numbers as the force balance — wheel radius, gear ratio, road speed — so a student can check them.

They get wrong: students treat a simulation result as a measurement. This is the one slide in the lecture that exists purely to inoculate against that. Do not skip it even when short on time — it costs 40 seconds.

Ask the room: what would you have to add to the model to get a believable top speed? (Torque as a function of engine speed. That is lecture 2 — and you have just written its first slide for me.)

In the model: The limiter block. Its ceiling is a constant, T_max. Nothing in this model knows the engine's speed, and that is exactly the assumption this slide is confessing.

Terms, if anyone asks

  • Ideal engine — one that delivers its peak torque at every speed. Ours does; a real one does not.
  • Torque map — torque as a function of crankshaft speed, Tmax(ω) instead of a constant. The fix, and lecture 2's subject.
  • Model validity — the range over which you are willing to believe it. Ours: honest to about 130 km/h, fiction above.

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • Crank speed — engine shaft speed in rpm, revolutions per minute. Road speed ÷ wheel radius gives wheel rad/s; times the gear ratio gives the crank.
  • 150 N·m — T_max, the constant ceiling in IdealEngine's limiter. The blue line.
  • ≈ 95 N·m — what the red shape gives at 8400 rpm. Indicative of a real engine, not of ours.
  • 3080 / 4450 / 8430 rpm — the crank speeds for 90, 130 and 246 km/h in this gearing.
  • 51 N·m — the most any demo in this lecture asks of the engine, on the 130 km/h climb in section 06.

Say: this procedure is four lines long and it is the foundation of notebook 08. Every heuristic tuning rule in this lecture reads its numbers off this one experiment.

Point at, on the torque plot: first the flat piece where the command has stepped and nothing has happened yet — that is dead time, the engine has not been told yet. Then the curve — the manifold filling. Two different delays with two different causes, and they need different treatment.

Say: this is also why we did not model an ideal engine. An engine with no lag makes this plant first order, and a first-order plant can never be driven to sustained oscillation — so Ziegler-Nichols in notebook 08 would be impossible, and Cohen-Coon would divide by a dead time of zero. The lags are load-bearing.

Ask the room: why step from 31 to 41 N·m rather than 0 to 150? (Because we want the response around the operating point we actually drive at. A huge step wanders across the nonlinearity in v².)

In the model: tau_lo and tau_hi are parameters, so the step test runs the equation the students just wrote down. Then IdealEngine.dyad: delay → lag → limiter, in that order.

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • T_90 = 31.1 N·m, T_110 = 40.1 N·m — the cruise torques for 90 and 110 km/h, derived from the force balance and handed to the model as parameters.
  • t_step = 50 s — when the command steps.
  • Commanded torque — cmd.y, what the controller asks for.
  • Delivered torque — plant.engine.limiter.y, what comes out of the engine after the delay, the lag and the limit.
  • Dead time — the flat gap at the start where the command has changed and nothing has happened. A pure delay.
  • Lag — the curve after it. A first-order response, not a delay: it starts immediately and approaches gradually.

Three names for one curve

Transient

The temporary part. The system's reaction to an abrupt change, which dies out.

Steady state

What is left after long enough that every startup effect has gone. The settled relationship between input and output.

Dynamic response

The whole journey — their sum.

$$ y(t) \;=\; y_{\text{transient}}(t) \;+\; y_{\text{steady-state}}(t) $$

Why the vocabulary matters today Proportional control is about to fail in steady state while looking fine in the transient. Without these three words that failure is invisible.

Point at the speed plot from the previous slide and mark the two regions with your hand: here it is moving, here it has stopped moving. Do it physically — this is the one piece of vocabulary everyone thinks they already have and half the room does not.

Ask the room: where exactly does the transient end? (It does not — it decays asymptotically. We pick a threshold, usually 2 % or 5 %, and that choice is ours, not the system's.)

Flag forward: every requirement you will ever be given is phrased in these terms — rise time and overshoot live in the transient, steady-state error lives in the other half.

Terms, if anyone asks

  • Transient response — the temporary part, which decays. Rise time, overshoot and settling time are measured here.
  • Steady-state response — what is left once the transient has died. Steady-state error is measured here.
  • Dynamic response — the sum of the two. The whole journey from one settled state to the next.
  • Settling time — when the response stays within a band, usually 2 % or 5 %, of its final value. The band is our choice, not the system's.

open notebook

K = 2.21 km/h per N·m  ·  τ = 63.0 s  ·  θ = 1.88 s

θ is not the engine's delay It is five times larger. Drag is quadratic, so the car's time constant changes across the step, and θ is the only parameter that can absorb that. The fit measures bend, not delay.

Read out all three values from the cell output, slowly, with units. Students will need them again in section 08 and they should write them down now.

Say: this is the whole of system identification for a first-order plant plus dead time — FOPTD. A step in, a curve out, three numbers off the curve. No free-body diagram, no equations of motion. If somebody handed you this car as a sealed box you could still have got here.

Do not skip the amber box, and do not let it embarrass you. A student who knows the engine's delay is 0.3 s will see 1.88 s and conclude the fit is broken. It is not. FOPTD has three parameters and our car is not FOPTD — it is a quadratic drag law, so its time constant is different at every speed. The fit spends θ on the mismatch because θ is the only parameter left.

Say: and this is what a practitioner would have. You do not get to know that your plant is really quadratic; you get a curve and three numbers. Notebook 08 tunes from these three exactly as they stand, and the controller works — which is the more interesting result.

Point at: the ratio θ/τ — about 0.030 here. That is the number every tuning rule in notebook 08 keys on, and it puts this car at the easy end of the range.

Honest framing, if asked: quote the tangent gain and time constant at each end of the step — 74.1 s at 90 km/h, 60.6 s at 110. They bracket the fit rather than pretending it is exact.

They get wrong: reading τ as "time to reach the final value". It is the time to reach 63 % of it. The helper uses the two-landmark construction from the tuning document, not an eyeball.

Readout — every symbol on this slide

  • K = 2.21 km/h per N·m — steady-state gain: how far the output moves per unit of input, once settled.
  • τ = 63.0 s — time constant: how long it takes to get there, dominated by the vehicle, not the engine.
  • θ = 1.878 s — dead time as the fit reports it. Not the engine's 0.3 s delay — see the amber box.
  • FOPTD — first order plus dead time, the three-parameter shape these numbers describe.
  • Tangent gain / time constant — the local values computed from the force balance at each end of the step: 74.1 s at 90 km/h, 60.6 s at 110. They bracket the fit honestly.
  • Secant gain — the total speed change divided by the total torque change, 20 ÷ 9.04. What K should land near.
