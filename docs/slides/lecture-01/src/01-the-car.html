The car

Before you can control something, you have to know what it does on its own.

What a system is

  • Control inputs u — what we deliberately move.
  • Disturbances d — what moves it anyway.
  • State x — what it remembers.
  • Output y — what we can measure.

For this car

ucommanded engine torque
droad gradient, wind, payload
xspeed (and, later, wheel speed)
yspeed at the speedometer, km/h

A system has structure, behavior and interconnectivity — parts that transform inputs into outputs.

Say: four boxes and you can describe any plant in this building. The only one students skip is d, and d is the reason feedback exists.

Point at: the x row. Right now the state is one number — speed. By the last notebook it is two, because the wheel gets to spin at its own rate, and that second state is what makes ice dangerous.

Ask the room: is the gradient an input or a disturbance? (Both, formally — it enters the same way. The difference is that we choose u and the hill chooses itself. That distinction is the whole lecture.)

The force balance

$$ m\,\frac{dv}{dt} \;=\; \underbrace{T\frac{i}{r}}_{\text{tractive}} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,C_dA\,v^2}_{O_V\ \text{drag}} \;-\; \underbrace{f_r\,m\,g\cos\alpha}_{O_f\ \text{rolling}} \;-\; \underbrace{m\,g\sin\alpha}_{O_s\ \text{slope}} $$

Acceleration resistance Oz is the left-hand side: the mass itself. Auxiliary loads Oaux are not modeled here.

Where the torque comes from T is not the commanded torque. Between command and shaft sit a transport delay and a first-order lag — the next slides are about why they are not optional.

Say: this is first-principles modeling — the white-box method. We are not fitting anything to data; we are writing down the physics we already believe, and then we will be able to argue about every term in it.

Point at: the drag term, v². That square is why this car is nearly linear around 100 km/h and not at all linear between 0 and 100.

They get wrong: the cosine on the rolling term. On a 10 % grade cos α = 0.995, so it looks ignorable — and it is, here. Say that you are keeping it because dropping terms is a decision, not an accident.

Tie back: slide 16 of the existing engine deck names these OV, Of, Oz, Os. Same equation, their notation.

Terminal speed, on paper first

notebooks/lecture01/01-the-car.ipynb cell 2. The force balance → Terminal speed 
c_drag = 0.5 * CAR.rho * CAR.CdA          # N per (m/s)^2
F_roll = CAR.f_r * CAR.m * CAR.g          # N, flat road
F_max  = CAR.T_max * CAR.i / CAR.r        # N, full torque through the driveline

v_terminal = sqrt((F_max - F_roll) / c_drag)          # m/s

Read out: 1935 newtons at the road, 165 of rolling resistance, and the speed where drag eats the rest — 68.4 m/s, 246 km/h.

Say: notice what we just did. We set the derivative to zero and solved. That is the steady-state model, and in two notebooks' time an integrator will find this same answer by itself, without being told any of this.

Ask the room, before you show the next slide: 246 km/h from a 150 N·m engine. Does anyone believe that? (Let the discomfort sit for a second — the next slide is the answer, and it lands much harder if they noticed first.)

246 km/h is wrong, and it is wrong on purpose

The model's honest limitation Our engine delivers its peak torque at every speed. A real one does not: peak torque lives in a band of a few thousand rpm and falls away on both sides.
  • Everything below 130 km/h — every demo in this lecture — is unaffected, because we never ask for more than about 40 N·m of the 150 available.
  • The top end is fiction. Say so now rather than let a student find it later and distrust the parts that are true.
  • Fixing it means an engine torque map — which is where lecture 2 starts.

Say: a model is a set of assumptions with a speed limit on it, and the engineering skill is knowing where that limit is. Ours is honest from 0 to about 130 km/h and a lie above it.

They get wrong: students treat a simulation result as a measurement. This is the one slide in the lecture that exists purely to inoculate against that. Do not skip it even when short on time — it costs 40 seconds.

Ask the room: what would you have to add to the model to get a believable top speed? (Torque as a function of engine speed. That is lecture 2 — and you have just written its first slide for me.)

Full throttle from rest

The step test

# settle for 500 s, step, then 500 s more
@named throttle_step = BlockComponents.Sources.Step(
    offset = T_90, height = T_110 - T_90, start_time = t_step)

sol_step = solve(ODEProblem(mtkcompile(step_test), [], (0.0, 1000.0)))

plot_torque(sol_step; commanded = "car_st.tau_cmd",
    delivered = "car_st.engine.limiter.y", limit = nothing,
    xlims = (t_step - 0.5, t_step + 3.0))
open notebook
  1. Wait until the process is at rest.
  2. Put the controller in manual.
  3. Change the control variable rapidly, and as large as noise allows.
  4. Record the response and scale it by the size of the step.

Say: this procedure is four lines long and it is the foundation of notebook 08. Every heuristic tuning rule in this lecture reads its numbers off this one experiment.

Point at, on the torque plot: first the flat piece where the command has stepped and nothing has happened yet — that is dead time, the engine has not been told yet. Then the curve — the manifold filling. Two different delays with two different causes, and they need different treatment.

Say: this is also why we did not model an ideal engine. An engine with no lag makes this plant first order, and a first-order plant can never be driven to sustained oscillation — so Ziegler-Nichols in notebook 08 would be impossible, and Cohen-Coon would divide by a dead time of zero. The lags are load-bearing.

Ask the room: why step from 31 to 41 N·m rather than 0 to 150? (Because we want the response around the operating point we actually drive at. A huge step wanders across the nonlinearity in v².)

Three names for one curve

Transient

The temporary part. The system's reaction to an abrupt change, which dies out.

Steady state

What is left after long enough that every startup effect has gone. The settled relationship between input and output.

Dynamic response

The whole journey — their sum.

$$ y(t) \;=\; y_{\text{transient}}(t) \;+\; y_{\text{steady-state}}(t) $$

Why the vocabulary matters today Proportional control is about to fail in steady state while looking fine in the transient. Without these three words that failure is invisible.

Point at the speed plot from the previous slide and mark the two regions with your hand: here it is moving, here it has stopped moving. Do it physically — this is the one piece of vocabulary everyone thinks they already have and half the room does not.

Ask the room: where exactly does the transient end? (It does not — it decays asymptotically. We pick a threshold, usually 2 % or 5 %, and that choice is ours, not the system's.)

Flag forward: every requirement you will ever be given is phrased in these terms — rise time and overshoot live in the transient, steady-state error lives in the other half.

θ is not the engine's 0.04 s delay It is forty times larger. Drag is quadratic, so the local time constant falls from 74.1 s at 90 km/h to 60.6 s at 110, and dead time is the only FOPTD parameter that can absorb that curvature. The fit measures bend, not delay.

Read out all three values from the cell output, slowly, with units. Students will need them again in section 08 and they should write them down now.

Say: this is the whole of system identification for a first-order plant plus dead time — FOPTD. A step in, a curve out, three numbers off the curve. No free-body diagram, no equations of motion. If somebody handed you this car as a sealed box you could still have got here.

Do not skip the amber box, and do not let it embarrass you. A student who knows the engine's delay is 0.04 s will see 1.63 s and conclude the fit is broken. It is not. FOPTD has three parameters and our car is not FOPTD — it is a quadratic drag law, so its time constant is different at every speed. The fit spends θ on the mismatch because θ is the only parameter left.

Say: and this is what a practitioner would have. You do not get to know that your plant is really quadratic; you get a curve and three numbers. Notebook 08 tunes from these three exactly as they stand, and the controller works — which is the more interesting result.

Point at: the ratio θ/τ — about 0.026 here. That is the number every tuning rule in notebook 08 keys on, and it puts this car at the easy end of the range.

Honest framing, if asked: quote the tangent gain and time constant at each end of the step — 74.1 s at 90 km/h, 60.6 s at 110. They bracket the fit rather than pretending it is exact.

They get wrong: reading τ as "time to reach the final value". It is the time to reach 63 % of it. The helper uses the two-landmark construction from the tuning document, not an eyeball.
