One diagram, and all of control theory hangs off it

Say: this one picture is the map for the whole subject, and for the next ninety minutes. Everything we build today is a change to one box in it.

Walk it with your hand, naming each signal:

  • r, the reference — what we want. Somebody has to decide it; that is planning, and for us it is just "the driver set 110".
  • u, the control input — what we deliberately do. Steering, brake, throttle.
  • d, the disturbances — what acts on the system anyway, and never asked us.
  • x, the state — what the system is actually doing.
  • y, the measurement — what we can see it doing, which is not the same thing, because every sensor adds noise.

Say: and note the shape of the argument. Feedback means the sensor's noise gets fed into the controller and therefore affects the true state. Noise in a feedback loop does not just make the picture fuzzy — it moves the car.

Ask the room: which of these five do you have direct control over? (Only u. Everything else is either chosen by somebody else or done to you.)

The rest of the subject, in one picture

Four areas, and a model in the middle of all of them — because you need one to design a controller, to estimate a state, to plan a path, and to check that any of it worked.

Which is why today starts where it does Notebook 01 builds the model and controls nothing. That is not a warm-up; it is the box everything else is wired to.

Today: the model, and the feedback half of "control". Planning is a driver pressing a button, and estimation appears only as the noise in section 07.

Say: nobody knows all of this well. But you should know the shape of it, because on a real project these are four different teams and you need to know whose problem you are looking at.

Say: and notice the middle box. Models are not an academic detour on the way to the real work — they are the thing every other box consumes. That is the justification for spending the first ten minutes of a control lecture with no controller in sight.

Say: feedback control itself is not one algorithm; it is a family — PID, full state feedback, gain scheduling, sliding mode, robust, adaptive, optimal, predictive, learned. We are doing the one that runs most of the machinery in the world, and the one whose three knobs you can reason about without a transfer function.

Ask the room: where does a self-driving car's route planner sit on this picture? (Top right. And it has to produce a reference the car can physically follow — which needs the model again.)

The forces on a car

Say: five forces, and the whole lecture's plant is the statement that they add up.

Point at each in turn, and give each one a physical sentence rather than a formula:

  • Tractive force, green, up the slope. Engine torque through the gear ratio, divided by the wheel radius. This is the only one we control.
  • Aerodynamic drag, red. Goes with the square of speed, so it is nothing at 30 km/h and it is most of the bill at 130.
  • Rolling resistance, amber. Roughly constant — about 165 newtons for this car, whatever the speed.
  • The slope term, blue. The part of the car's own weight that points down the road. On a 10 % grade that is about 1.4 kilonewtons — far more than drag and rolling put together, which is why section 06's hill beats the engine.
  • The inertia term. Whatever is left over goes into accelerating the mass. If the others balance, there is nothing left and the speed is steady.

Ask the room: at 90 km/h on the flat, which is bigger — drag or rolling resistance? (Drag, by about a factor of one and a half. Ask them again for 30 km/h and the answer flips. This is the square in action and it is worth the thirty seconds.)

Tie back to the course deck: these are the OV, Of, Oz, Os from the energy slides — same terms, drawn on a car instead of listed. Oaux, the electrical loads, we are leaving out; say so, because leaving a term out should always be a decision you announce.

Two conventions, for the whole lecture

The loop runs in km/h

Setpoint, measurement and error are all km/h. So every gain you see today has the same units and means the same thing:

k is in N·m per km/h.

"Five newton-metres for every km/h I am off." That is a sentence an engineer can sanity-check; k = 220 in mixed units is not.

SI inside, km/h at the sensor

The plant's internals are SI throughout. Exactly one conversion exists in the whole model, and it is a visible block on the sensor output:

Vehicle.ToKmPerHour

No notebook ever multiplies a plotted signal by 3.6. If a speed axis says km/h it is because the model emitted km/h.

Say: two housekeeping rules, and then we start. Both exist so that a number on a plot means one thing only.

Why it matters: the most common way a student's cruise controller "mysteriously" misbehaves is a gain tuned against m/s driving a loop that measures km/h — a silent factor of 3.6. Put the conversion in the model where you can see it, once.

Ask the room: if I tell you my gain is 220, what is missing? (Units. Wait for it — someone will say it.)
