Step 10 · 20 min

Standing start on a slippery road

Floor it on ice: the wheel spins, the car barely moves.

page: step 10 · reference dyad/HandsOn/SlipCar.dyad

Say: everything in this car is a part you already have. The new thing is wiring: the tire is the contact now, so there is no translational ground — the wheel's flange drives the body directly.

Timing: 20 minutes — 13 parts and 15 wires. The page lists the wires as a numbered table; tell the room to tick them off. Create the car, then its parameters, then the parts, then the wires, then a separate bench.

Two speeds out

Point at: r is used twice — the tire's radius and the km/h gain — and m sets both the body's mass and the axle load. One parameter each, so they cannot disagree. Typed twice, a change to one is a silent bug in the other.

Say: wheel surface speed is ω·r. Put on the same axis as the car's speed, wheelspin is simply the gap between two lines.

They get wrong: missing initial car.wheel_inertia.w = 0. With slip, wheel speed is no longer tied to car speed, so it needs its own start.

1500 km/h at the tire, 10 km/h on the road

Say: past the peak of the curve, more slip gives less grip. The wheel speeds up, grip falls, it speeds up more. The model has no rev limit, so the engine keeps pouring 150 N·m into a wheel with nothing to push against — 1500 km/h is the model telling you the truth about an assumption, not a bug.

Then the dry run: friction__k = 1.0 overrides the constant without editing the model. Twice the speed of the icy car, and the two lines stay together.

Close: notebook 10 of the lecture is this car under cruise control on an ice patch. The controller sees speed falling, commands more torque, and does exactly what you just watched. That is the argument for traction control — and for the cascade control of lecture 2.
