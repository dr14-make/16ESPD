From a twist to a push: F = T · i / r

Step 1 — the gear multiplies the twist

The engine turns i = 4 times for every turn of the wheel. Like a low bicycle gear: easier to turn, so the wheel gets 4 × the torque (and turns 4 × slower).

$$ T_{\text{wheel}} = i \cdot T $$

Step 2 — the wheel turns the twist into a push

Torque is force times lever arm. At the road the lever arm is the wheel radius r, so Twheel = F · r:

$$ F = \frac{T_{\text{wheel}}}{r} = \frac{T\,i}{r} $$

With i = 4.0 and r = 0.31 m

1290 N on 1400 kg, nothing else acting: a = F/m = 0.92 m/s² — step 03's check.

Speeds go the other way

Wheel speed = v / r; engine speed = i × that. At 90 km/h: 25 m/s ÷ 0.31 × 4 = 323 rad/s = 3080 rpm.

Say: two steps, both things everyone has felt. A bicycle in a low gear: you pedal fast, the wheel turns slowly, and climbing is easy — the gear trades speed for force. And a spanner: the longer the handle, the less force you need for the same twist. The wheel is a spanner the other way round: the twist at the axle becomes a force at the rim, and a bigger wheel means less force.

Derivation, step by step

  1. Gear: torque × i, speed ÷ i. An ideal gear loses no energy, so power in = power out: T·ωengine = Twheel·ωwheel. With ωengine = i·ωwheel, that gives Twheel = i·T.
  2. Wheel: torque = force × radius, so the force at the road is F = Twheel / r.
  3. Together: F = T·i / r.

The numbers: 150 × 4 = 600 N·m at the wheel; 600 / 0.31 = 1935 N. 100 × 4 / 0.31 = 1290 N; 1290 / 1400 = 0.921 m/s², which the step-03 reference test checks.

Engine speed, for the "246 km/h is wrong" slide later: the wheel rolls without sliding, so one turn moves the car 2πr. Wheel angular speed ω = v / r = 25 / 0.31 = 80.6 rad/s. Engine: × 4 = 322.6 rad/s. To rpm: × 60 / 2π = 3080 rpm.

They get wrong: "150 N·m is a weak engine". Do the multiplication out loud: almost 2 kN at the road, about the weight of 200 kg.
