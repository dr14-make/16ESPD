# Building Lecture 3's brake models in three steps

A passenger-car brake system can be modeled end to end with a few lumped states per stage, and each of the lecture's three steps maps onto a model already validated in the literature. The chain works like this. The pedal lever multiplies foot force about 3.5×. A vacuum booster multiplies it about 5× more until its rear chamber reaches atmosphere. A quasi-static tandem master cylinder turns the pushrod force into roughly equal pressure in two circuits. That pressure pushes about **3.6 cm³** of fluid through the lines into calipers whose pressure-volume curve stiffens as they fill. The calipers convert pressure to torque at about **69 N·m/bar** for the repository's lumped 1400 kg car, so the dry-road friction limit (4256 N·m) sits near **62 bar** and the existing 6000 N·m demand near **87 bar**, which is right at booster run-out. The 1997 California PATH model (Gerdes, Hedrick et al.) gives a three-state, test-car-validated backbone for steps 1 and 2: two booster chamber pressures plus one displaced fluid volume. The Delphi and MathWorks references supply the intake-manifold and check-valve pieces. For step 3, the Bosch eight-phase ABS cycle maps one-to-one onto inlet and outlet valve commands. The repository's `ABSModulator` phase table can therefore drive valves in place of a torque integrator, and its "never exceed driver demand" clip becomes a physical property of the valves. The main recommendation is to build all three steps as **causal, signal-level Dyad components** (volume nodes publish pressure, restrictions publish flow), size the hydraulic time constants so that none falls below about 1 ms, and keep the explicit solver the tire model prefers. Several numbers in the research notes needed correction. The PATH booster flow coefficients only reproduce measured response times if read per kPa rather than per Pa, and the notes' hose compliance is ten times too small.

## Force is multiplied twice before any fluid moves

The pedal is a rigid lever: `F_rod = i_p·F_ped` and `x_rod = x_ped/i_p`. Aftermarket sources put boosted pedal ratios at **4.5–5:1** and unboosted ratios at **6–7:1** ([Summit Racing](https://help.summitracing.com/knowledgebase/article/SR-05037/en-us)). One trade source gives 3.2–4:1 for OEM boosted systems ([Tomorrow's Technician](https://www.tomorrowstechnician.com/boosters-and-master-cylinders-how-pressure-builds/)). The regulatory ceiling on driver effort is **500 N**, and FMVSS 135 also requires six stops within that force with the power assist depleted ([49 CFR 571.135](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/571.135)). That test is why a booster failure costs deceleration but not the brakes.

The vacuum booster is a diaphragm splitting a front (vacuum) chamber, always connected to the vacuum source through a check valve, from a rear (working) chamber. A poppet valve in the power-piston hub has three states, set by where the input plunger sits relative to the piston. Released: the vacuum port is open and both chambers are at vacuum. Apply: the vacuum port closes and atmosphere enters the rear chamber. Hold (lap): both ports are closed ([EduMech](https://www.edumech.co.uk/learn/braking/vacuum-brake-booster); [Bosch](https://www.bosch-mobility.com/en/solutions/driving-safety/vacuum-brake-booster/)). A rubber reaction disc divides the output reaction between plunger and piston. The ratio of those contact areas, not the diaphragm size, sets the **servo ratio**. The initial clearance between disc and plunger produces the **jump-in** step ([US5943937](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5943937)).

The static characteristic therefore has five pieces: a dead zone up to the cut-in force, a vertical jump-in, a linear boosted slope equal to the servo ratio, a knee at **run-out** when the rear chamber reaches atmospheric pressure, and a slope of 1 beyond it ([US6033038A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6033038A/en)). The release branch lies below the apply branch because of the valve dead band. Maximum assist is `A_d·(p_atm − p_front) − F_rs`. With the PATH test car's **A_d = 0.0533 m²** diaphragm (about 10.3 in) and **33 kPa abs** idle vacuum, that is about **3.5 kN** ([PATH UCB-ITS-PRR-97-21](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/02b8f7q2)). Run-out scales linearly with `p_atm − p_manifold`, so altitude or a weak vacuum moves the knee to a lower pedal force. No primary source gave production values for servo ratio, jump-in or cut-in. The commonly quoted 3:1–8:1 range comes only from a blog ([PatSnap](https://eureka.patsnap.com/blog/machinery-tech-resources/what-is-brake-booster/)), so the model defaults below mark them as assumed.

Where the vacuum comes from depends on the powertrain. A throttled spark-ignition engine makes it for free. Diesels and unthrottled gasoline engines (Valvetronic, MultiAir) need a separate pump, and the check valve holds stored vacuum at full load and after the engine stops ([de.wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremskraftverst%C3%A4rker)). Hybrids and EVs use electric vane pumps such as the Hella UP-series, which the ECU switches from a booster pressure sensor ([Hella tech info](https://www.evwest.com/support/SC-VP-Hella-UP28-Vacuum-Pump-Technical-Information.pdf)). Alternatively they drop vacuum altogether for an electromechanical booster: the Bosch iBooster delivers up to **6.2 kN** from a motor of up to 450 W, builds pressure three times faster than ESP, and adds regenerative blending and redundancy ([Bosch iBooster](https://www.bosch-mobility.com/en/solutions/driving-safety/ibooster/)).

The tandem master cylinder puts two pistons in one bore. The pushrod drives the primary piston, and primary pressure plus an intermediate spring drive the floating secondary. In steady state both circuits carry `p ≈ F_rod/A_mc`, less spring preloads worth tenths of a bar. Before pressure rises, each piston must cover its compensating port or close its central valve ([US20080282697A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080282697A1/en)). PATH's quasi-static form is the one to use: `p_mc = (F_out − F_cs − F_cf)/A_mc`, with **A_mc = 4.91 cm²** (25 mm bore), a spring preload of **138 N** and seal friction of **80 N** ([PATH](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/02b8f7q2)). Pedal travel is then kinematic: `x_rod = s_dead + V_displaced/A_mc`. The two circuits are split either front/rear (II) or diagonally (X). An X split always keeps one front and one opposite rear brake, about half the capability, but pulls the car toward the working front wheel ([EduMech](https://www.edumech.co.uk/learn/braking/split-divided-braking); [US10988170](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10988170)). The repository's single lumped wheel cannot show yaw, so the lecture can show the split only as lost deceleration and extra pedal travel.

## Sixty-two bar and four cubic centimeters stop the car

Brake fluid is almost incompressible next to everything around it. DOT 4 has a density of about **1.045 kg/L** ([Liqui Moly](https://www.liqui-moly.com/en/us/brake-fluid-dot-4-p000420.html)) and a compressibility of about **7×10⁻⁴ MPa⁻¹** (bulk modulus about 1.4 GPa) at room temperature, rising roughly fourfold at 200 °C ([ScienceDirect/Day](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/brake-fluid)). Viscosity is the parameter that matters. DOT 4 may reach **1800 mm²/s at −40 °C**, DOT 4 LV is capped at **750 mm²/s**, and the floor is 1.5 mm²/s at 100 °C ([Wikipedia: Brake fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brake_fluid); [Brake & Front End](https://www.brakeandfrontend.com/what-is-a-dot-4-lv-brake-fluid-video/)).

A laminar line obeys `Δp = 128·μ·L·Q/(π·D⁴)` ([Lundin, KTH](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:818797/FULLTEXT01.pdf)). For a 3 mm bore at an assumed 10 mm²/s at 20 °C, that gives about **0.08 bar·s/cm³** for a 1.5 m front run and **0.21 bar·s/cm³** for a 4 m rear run. The resulting RC time constants are 1–10 ms. At −40 °C they grow 180-fold, to seconds for the rear circuit, which is the reason the LV grade exists and makes a strong lecture demonstration. Fluid inertance puts a line resonance near 17 Hz and the acoustic delay is 3–4 ms. Both matter for pedal feel and ABS pressure ripple but can be dropped for deceleration studies.

Most of the fluid a brake takes goes into the wheel brake's compliance, not into compressing the fluid. The pressure-volume curve starts with a nearly flat take-up of pad clearance and then stiffens progressively, because "the wheel brake stiffens as volume grows" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/brake-fluid)). Production ECUs store it as a dead stroke plus at least six P–V points for fault detection ([US9656653](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9656653)). Seal deformation contributes almost nothing, **under 2 mm³ over 0–50 bar** for a 57 mm piston ([ScienceDirect/Day](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/brake-fluid)). The compliance is in the pads, the caliper body and the hoses. SAE J1401 allows a 1/8-in hose to expand up to **0.33 cm³/ft at 69 bar**, and good hoses reach about 0.15 ([US7748412](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7748412)). For a 0.4 m wheel hose that is about **3–6 mm³/bar**. The research notes' 0.3–0.6 mm³/bar is an arithmetic slip, and at 60 bar four hoses can hold 0.7–1.5 cm³, a first-order share. No verified production caliper curve was found, so the P–V defaults below are shaped to textbook magnitudes and marked assumed.

Disc torque per wheel is `T = 2·μ_pad·max(0, p − p₀)·A_pist·r_eff`, with μ_pad ≈ 0.35–0.45 and a push-out pressure p₀ below 1 bar. Drum brakes have a larger brake factor. A duo-servo drum gives a high braking effect but is "sensitive to variation in lining μ" ([US6186294](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6186294); [Akebono](https://www.akebono-brake.com/english/product_technology/product/automotive/drum/)), which is why cars moved to discs. With 57 mm front and 38 mm rear floating calipers, μ_pad = 0.40 and effective radii of 0.12/0.11 m (textbook values, unverified), the gains are **49.0 N·m/bar for the front axle and 19.9 N·m/bar for the rear**. That is **68.9 N·m/bar** in total and a 71/29 installed split.

Front/rear distribution is where the lecture can show why proportioning exists. With load transfer, the ideal front share is `φ_f(z) = (l_r + z·h)/l`. For l = 2.6 m, 60 % static front load and h = 0.55 m (assumed), the ideal share rises from 0.60 at rest to 0.81 at 1 g. The installed 71/29 equals the ideal only at **z_crit = (φ_inst·l − l_r)/h ≈ 0.52 g**. Harder than that, the rear would lock first. A proportioning valve bends the rear curve at a knee of **41–48 bar** and passes about **43 %** of further input ([Brakes-shop Brakepedia](https://www.brakes-shop.com/brakepedia/general/brake-proportioning-valves); [Wilwood](https://shop.wilwood.com/blogs/news/how-does-a-proportioning-valve-work)). Modern cars do the same thing electronically (EBD) by holding the rear inlet valves ([US6595600](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6595600)). These knee and slope values come from aftermarket sources, not OEM data.

The whole chain checks out numerically. At 62 bar the pushrod needs about 3.3 kN. With an assumed servo ratio of 5, jump-in of 500 N and cut-in of 40 N, the booster input is about 590 N and the foot force about **170 N** at a 3.5:1 pedal. The fluid demand is about 3.6 cm³, so master-cylinder stroke is about 10 mm including dead travel and pedal travel about 34 mm. Booster run-out falls near **88 bar**, almost exactly the repository's 6000 N·m demand. PATH's measured step response supports the time scales: master-cylinder pressure starts rising about **0.08 s** after a 200 N step, and wheel pressure settles in **0.4–0.5 s** ([PATH Fig. 2.12](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/02b8f7q2)).

## The booster borrows air from a one-state manifold

The standard mean-value model of an SI intake manifold has one state, `dp_m/dt = (R·T_m/V_m)·(ṁ_thr + ṁ_bst − ṁ_cyl)`. Throttle flow follows the isentropic orifice law `ṁ_thr = C_d·A_thr·p_a/√(R·T_a)·Ψ(p_m/p_a)` and chokes below a pressure ratio of 0.528. MathWorks linearizes it above Π = 0.95 to avoid the infinite slope at Δp → 0 ([MathWorks Flow Restriction](https://www.mathworks.com/help/autoblks/ref/flowrestriction.html)). Cylinder flow is speed-density, `ṁ_cyl = η_v·V_d·N/120·p_m/(R·T_m)` ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/engine-volumetric-efficiency)). Hendricks' SAE 960037 and Guzzella and Onder are the reference texts ([SAE 960037](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/modelling-intake-manifold-filling-dynamics-960037)). MathWorks' Air Intake block defaults are a usable parameter set: V_m = 1 L, V_d = 1.5 L, a 50 mm throttle and 298 K ([MathWorks Air Intake](https://www.mathworks.com/help/sdl/ref/airintake.html)). That block writes cylinder flow as `ω·V_d·p/(2RT)`, which overstates flow by 2π if ω is in rad/s. Use the rpm form above.

At idle the throttle is choked, so manifold pressure is set by the idle air area against engine pumping. A hot idle reads about **27 kPa** MAP and 3 g/s of airflow ([MOTOR](https://www.motor.com/magazine-summary/fuel-injection-diagnosis-july-2008/)). Idle vacuum is typically **16–20 inHg**, overrun reads 4–5 inHg more, and wide-open throttle drops to zero ([AA1Car](https://www.aa1car.com/library/map_sensors.htm)). Hitting PATH's 33 kPa at 800 rpm with η_v = 0.8 requires an effective idle area of about **13 mm²** (C_d = 1). MathWorks' 1 cm² leakage default would flood the manifold at idle, so the idle area should be calibrated rather than copied. The linearized manifold time constant `τ_m = 120·V_m/(η_v·V_d·N)` is about **0.125 s at 800 rpm**. At altitude the idle MAP stays about 27 kPa, but the available vacuum falls from 74 to 57 kPa at 5500 ft ([MOTOR](https://www.motor.com/magazine-summary/fuel-injection-diagnosis-july-2008/)), and booster run-out falls in proportion.

The booster's dynamics are two ideal-gas chambers whose volumes change with piston travel, linked by three flows: atmosphere to rear through the atmospheric valve, rear to front through the vacuum valve, and front to manifold through the check valve. Delphi writes these as `dP_a/dt = [R·γ·T·(ṁ₁ψ₁ − ṁ₂ψ₂) − γ·P_a·A_b·Ẋ_p]/V_a`, plus the matching vacuum-chamber equation, with switches ψᵢ for valve and check-valve state ([US6033038A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6033038A/en)). Gerdes and Hedrick use a static (memoryless) valve with force thresholds and linearized flows. They validated it on a car, and it reproduces "both static hysteresis effects and rapid transients" ([ASME JDSMC 1999](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/dynamicsystems/article-abstract/121/3/496/395112/Brake-System-Modeling-for-Simulation-and-Control)).

PATH Table 2.1 supplies the full set: **C_aa = 5.8×10⁻⁵, C_av = 2.2×10⁻⁴, C_vm = 1.26×10⁻⁴, C_leak = 1.4×10⁻⁷**. It also gives a front chamber of 2.4 L, a rear chamber of 0.43 L at rest, a return spring of 97 N + 2411 N/m, and valve thresholds **F_app = F_rel = 50 N** ([PATH](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/02b8f7q2)). The table labels the flow coefficients m·s, meaning ṁ = C·Δp in Pa. Read that way, they give a rear-chamber time constant of about 0.2 ms, three orders of magnitude faster than PATH's own traces. Read as **kg/(s·kPa)**, the same numbers give `τ = V/(R·T·C) ≈ 0.21 s` for the rear chamber and 0.23 s for the front. That matches the measured 0.1–0.4 s rise and the front chamber's recovery from 38 to 34 kPa after a stop. The lecture should use the per-kPa reading and state why.

The booster also disturbs the engine. Filling the rear chamber for one stop takes about **0.8 g** of air at 10 mm of piston travel (computed from PATH geometry). On release that air goes to the manifold, about a quarter of a second of idle airflow, which appears as a MAP bump. With the check valve closed (wide-open throttle, boost, engine off), each full apply-and-release mixes rear-chamber air into the front chamber. The computed reserve falls from 3.6 kN to 2.4 kN to 1.6 kN of maximum assist over successive stops, which reproduces the service rule of "2 to 3 power-assisted applications" ([Brake & Front End](https://www.brakeandfrontend.com/ase-a5-how-to-test-vacuum-power-assist-and-hydroboost-braking-systems/)). An electric pump fits in as `ṁ = (Q/(R·T))·max(p_f − p_min, 0)·u`. Hella's UP28, which evacuates a 3.2 L test volume to 500 mbar in 5.5–6 s and pulls at least 86 % vacuum, implies **Q ≈ 0.40 L/s** and p_min ≈ 0.14·p_a ([Hella brief](https://www.hella.com/resources-soe/assets/documents_global/10069076a_AM0.pdf)). Aftermarket pumps switch on below about 18 inHg and off above about 25 inHg ([Summit Racing](https://www.summitracing.com/search/part-type/vacuum-pumps-street)). No check-valve cracking pressure was found. Zero is the honest default.

## The ABS modulator can only build, hold or dump

Each wheel channel of a return-flow ABS unit has a normally-open inlet valve from the master cylinder and a normally-closed outlet valve to a low-pressure accumulator (LPA). Normal braking has the inlet open and the outlet closed. On incipient lock the inlet closes (hold), and if the wheel keeps decelerating the outlet opens (dump) ([Cai et al., EMEIT-2012](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/3621.pdf)). The dumped fluid first fills a spring-loaded piston accumulator, which allows an immediate pressure drop. A reciprocating pump, one per circuit on a shared eccentric-driven motor, then returns it to the master-cylinder side ([US5590936A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5590936A/en); [US5199860A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5199860A/en)). That returned fluid pushes the master-cylinder piston back and the driver feels it as pedal kickback ([Underhood Service](https://www.underhoodservice.com/abs-esc-hydraulic-control-unit-modulator-diagnostics/)). An ESP unit adds a switchover valve and a high-pressure suction valve per circuit, 12 valves in total ([ResearchGate, Bosch 5.7](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Diagram-of-the-ESP-electrohydraulic-modulator-ABS-Bosch-57-RREV-RRAV-LFEV-LFAV_fig1_326685570)).

The LPA is preloaded to **4–6 bar** and reads about **10 bar** when full ([US5015043A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5015043A/en)). Cai et al. use **5.3 ml** with a 24 mm piston. They also study valve seats of **0.74–1.16 mm** and give the solenoid and armature equations, but a lumped model does not need the armature. A first-order opening lag of a few ms is enough, inferred from the 5–10 ms switching of direct-acting solenoids ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solenoid_valve)). Cai's printed pump piston diameter of "1 mm" yields 1.6 mm³ per revolution, too small to empty the accumulator, and is evidently a typo. Pump displacement therefore has to be assumed. Once the LPA is full its pressure climbs toward wheel pressure and dump capability collapses to the pump-out rate, which is why sustained low-μ ABS needs the pump running. Valves are orifices, `q = C_d·A·√(2/ρ)·Δp/(Δp² + Δp_cr²)^¼`, which is turbulent at large Δp and laminar near zero. Production analog inlet valves are driven with a current taken from stored opening-current curves against Δp ([US8215722B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8215722B2/en)). Held partly open, they meter a gradual rise in wheel pressure ([US10118600](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10118600)). That justifies modeling slow build as a duty-averaged opening rather than as literal pulses.

The Bosch control cycle, as re-implemented by EDC from Bosch's *Driving-safety systems*, runs in eight phases. Follow the master cylinder until wheel deceleration passes −a. Hold until slip passes a threshold. Dump until the wheel re-accelerates. Hold for the apply delay or until +A. Build at the primary rate. Hold. Build slowly at the secondary rate, about 1/10 of primary. Then dump again. Its defaults are a primary build of **345 bar/s**, a secondary of **34.5 bar/s**, a **50 ms** apply delay, −a = −175 rad/s² and +a = 50 rad/s² ([Day & Roberts, SAE 2002-01-0559](https://edccorp.com/library/TechRefPdfs/EDC-0033.pdf)). The paper states the release rate both as 10 000 psi/s (690 bar/s) and as 7000 kPa/s, so one of the two is a typo. The repository's existing `ABSModulator` phase table is a compressed version of the same cycle (DECREASE, pre-HOLD, recovering-HOLD, INCREASE slow, INCREASE fast). Its torque rates translate at 68.9 N·m/bar into **580 bar/s** for release and fast build and **116 bar/s** for slow build. That is the same order as EDC's figures, so reproducing them with valves keeps the torque-level and hydraulic ABS directly comparable.

One tension could not be resolved from the sources. Cai's 1.0 mm seats, at C_d = 0.65, 55 bar Δp and the stiffened caliper compliance near 60 bar, give on the order of **10⁴ bar/s**, 15–30 times the EDC defaults. Matching 580 bar/s needs an equivalent seat of about 0.3 mm per wheel. The sources do not say whether real units are that fast and pulse more briefly, or whether EDC's defaults are simply conservative. The model should calibrate its default seat area to the spec rates and expose the seat diameter so students can see the sensitivity.

## Signals beat ports for this lecture, and nothing needs a stiff solver

The Dyad stdlib's `HydraulicComponents` lacks the orifice, valve, check valve, accumulator and pump that steps 1 and 3 need. Its volume element is built for a bulk-modulus fluid, whereas the dominant compliance here is a nonlinear caliper P–V curve. No pneumatic library is available for step 2 at all. An acausal build would mean first writing a hydraulic and a pneumatic connector family and then every element on top of them. The payoff would be rewiring by drag and drop: II versus X, circuit failure, adding the modulator. The one-wheel plant uses little of that. The recommended approach is causal and signal-level, with one discipline borrowed from bond graphs. **Every capacitance component owns a volume state and publishes pressure. Every resistance component takes two pressures and publishes a flow.** Every node's equation is then `dV/dt = Σ q_in − Σ q_out`, and there are no algebraic loops. Students see the same R–C alternation they would see on an acausal diagram. Switching uses `ifelse`, `min` and `max` (all already used in `BrakeActuator`). Memory, where it is needed (ABS latches, pump hysteresis), uses the continuous-latch pattern already specified in `docs/abs-controller-spec.md`. An acausal port library is worth building later, when the 2D four-wheel vehicle arrives and topology starts to vary.

On stiffness, the time scales the model actually needs are:

| Element | Time constant | Keep it? |
|---|---|---|
| Booster chambers (PATH, per-kPa reading) | 0.2 s | yes, sets driver-apply lag |
| Intake manifold at idle | 0.03–0.13 s | yes |
| Brake line RC at 20 °C | 1–10 ms (smallest at high pressure) | yes, but floor it at ≥ 1 ms |
| ABS valve opening lag | 3 ms (assumed) | yes |
| Orifice laminar region near Δp = 0 | `C_w·√Δp_cr/(C_d·A·√(2/ρ))`, sub-ms if Δp_cr is tiny | set Δp_cr ≈ 1–5 bar |
| Line inertance (17 Hz) and acoustic delay (3–4 ms) | — | drop |
| Solenoid armature, pump pistons | sub-ms | drop, use averaged pump flow |

With the smallest retained constant at about 1 ms, an explicit Tsit5 run over a 6 s stop takes on the order of 10⁴ steps. That is acceptable, and it avoids the BDF trouble `SlipWheel1D` documents. If a parameter sweep pushes step counts past about 10⁵ (cold fluid is the opposite case, since it slows things down), raise Δp_cr or switch that run to a Rosenbrock method. The existing `BrakeActuator` lag (T = 0.03 s) conflated two different physical time scales: driver apply, dominated by the booster at hundreds of ms, and ABS modulation, dominated by valves and lines at ms to tens of ms. Once hydraulics supply both, that lag should be shrunk rather than stacked on top.

## Recommended staged models for Lecture 3

The plan below keeps `BrakedWheel`, `BrakeActuator`, `SlipWheel1D` and `IdealEngine` unchanged and adds components under a new `dyad/Vehicle/Brake/` sublibrary, following the `Wheel/` layout. Hydraulics connect to the wheel through one new signal block. `CaliperTorque` feeds `BrakedWheel.tau_cmd`, and the harness sets `BrakedWheel(T_brake = 0.005, tau_max = 1e5)`. With those parameters the actuator lag becomes pad and caliper mechanics and the saturation never binds. That fits the spec's "do not change" rule, because both are existing parameters. Units inside the brake components are SI. Pressures in bar and volumes in cm³ below are for readability only.

### Step 1: a hydraulic circuit that explains the system (3 hydraulic states)

| Component | States | Equations | Defaults (source) | Demonstrates |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| `StaticBooster` | none | `F_out = 0` if F_in < F_cut; else `min(F_jump + SR·(F_in − F_cut), F_in + F_assist,max)`; `F_assist,max = A_d·(p_atm − p_vac) − F_rs0` | SR = 5, F_cut = 40 N, F_jump = 500 N (assumed); A_d = 0.0533 m², p_vac = 33 kPa, F_rs0 = 97 N (PATH) | jump-in, boost, run-out near 88 bar |
| `TandemMasterCylinder` | `V_disp` (integrator only) | `p_mc = max(0, F_out − F_cs0 − F_cf)/A_mc`; `dV_disp/dt = q_f + q_r`; `x_rod = s_dead + V_disp/A_mc` | A_mc = 4.91 cm², F_cs0 = 138 N, F_cf = 80 N (PATH); s_dead = 2.5 mm (assumed, 1.5–3.5 textbook) | equal circuit pressures, pedal travel from fluid demand |

Rerun the spec's acceptance criteria 1–6 on the hydraulic plant at road_mu ∈ {1.0, 0.5, 0.2}, with the torque-level ABS overlaid on the same plot. Criterion 1 ("never exceed demand") now holds by construction, because an inlet valve cannot raise wheel pressure above master-cylinder pressure. Stating that is one of the lecture's clearest points. Two tests are new. First, a negative test: at μ = 0.2 with the pump disabled, the LPA fills, dumping stops working, and the wheel locks. Second, check that pedal travel `x_ped` shows the kickback during cycling. The spec listed a pressure-domain model, valve PWM and EBD as out of scope. Step 3 brings the first two into scope. EBD still needs per-axle slip, so it waits for the 2D vehicle.

## Conclusion

The research changes the lecture's story in one important way. The repository's brake has been a single 30 ms lag. The physical brake has two separate paths. The driver path is slow: pneumatic, about 0.2 s, dependent on vacuum, and saturating at run-out right where this car's 6000 N·m demand sits. The ABS path is fast: hydraulic, milliseconds, with rates set by the valves, and it can only lower what the driver requested. Modeling them separately is what makes the ABS constraint physical rather than a clip, and what makes the engine matter to braking at all, through wide-open throttle, altitude, diesels and EVs.

Treat the parameter set as partly calibrated. The PATH booster and Hella pump numbers are anchored to measurements once the unit ambiguity is resolved. The caliper P–V curve, servo ratio, jump-in, pump displacement and valve seat area are not, and the Cai-versus-EDC gap in valve rates is an order of magnitude. The most useful single upgrade is to open the MathWorks "ABS Open Loop Test Bench" and "Vacuum Boosted Tandem Primary Cylinder" models in MATLAB, or Breuer and Bill's handbook, and harvest a consistent caliper curve and valve sizing to replace the assumed values.
