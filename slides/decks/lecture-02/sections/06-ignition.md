# Ignition

When to fire the spark, what it does to torque and emissions, and how the high voltage is made and
delivered.

<!--
The second actuator. Fuel decides how much energy is in the cylinder; the spark decides when it is
released, and so how much of it becomes work. We start with timing, then the parts: plug, coil,
and the way the voltage reaches the plug.

## Ignition timing

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.25fr_1fr] gap-6">
<div>

- **Ignition timing** is a measurement, in degrees of crankshaft rotation, of the point at which
  the spark is fired in each cylinder.
- **Advanced ignition**: ignition before the piston reaches top dead center (TDC).
- At idle the spark fires a few degrees **BTDC**; the advance increases with engine speed and
  decreases with load.
- **Why bother?**
  - the fuel does not burn completely the instant the spark fires
  - the combustion gases take time to expand
  - the time for burning and expansion is fixed, so the crank angle it takes depends on engine speed
- **Ignition timing affects**
  - engine lifetime · fuel economy · engine power

</div>
<div>

<CardExport notebook="illustrations" name="advance-curves" height="25rem" />

<div class="caption">Centrifugal plus vacuum advance, 1 part load, 2 full load; right, the vacuum unit. Idealized, drawn from textbook trends</div>

</div>
</div>

<!--
The flame takes roughly the same time to cross the chamber whatever the engine speed, a few
milliseconds. At idle, a few milliseconds is a few degrees of crank. At 6000 rpm it is many more.
So the faster the engine turns, the earlier the spark must fire for the peak pressure to land at
the same crank angle, just after top dead center.

Load matters too. At part load the manifold pressure is low, the charge is thin and burns slowly,
so it needs more advance. That is curve 1, which has more advance than full load, curve 2. In old
engines the vacuum in the manifold drove that extra advance directly: the lower plot is the
vacuum along each curve, the right plot what the vacuum unit adds for it. Full load has almost no
vacuum, so curve 2 is the centrifugal advance alone: 7 degrees at idle, 27 from 5500 rpm. Part
load peaks near 36 degrees around 4000 rpm.

The plot is idealized, drawn from textbook trends: the shapes of the Bosch figure the source deck
scanned, with the vacuum unit's 13 degrees an assumed value that reproduces it. The source slide
said "about 5 degrees BTDC" for normal operation; idle timing is a few degrees, 7 in the figure.
Dyad task 6 replaces the plot with the MBT advance its single-cylinder sweep finds at part and full
load.

## Spark plug

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.6fr_1fr] gap-6">
<div class="text-[0.95rem]">

- **Function**: the spark plug introduces the ignition energy generated by the ignition coil into
  the combustion chamber.
- **Operation**: the high voltage creates an electric spark between the spark-plug electrodes.
- **Requirements**: it must work under extreme conditions (cold starting, full load)
  - ignition voltages of up to **30,000 V**
  - temperatures in the region of **1000 °C**
- **Electrode gap**
  - as large as possible, so that the spark activates a large volume of mixture
  - narrow enough that the ignition voltage still produces a reliable spark

</div>
<div class="grid grid-cols-[1fr_1.2fr] gap-3 items-center">
<div>

![Spark plug in section.](/pptx/slide48-spark-plug-section.png)

</div>
<div class="text-xs">

1. high-voltage connector (terminal nut)
2. Al₂O₃ ceramic insulator
3. shell
4. heat-shrinkage zone
5. conductive glass
6. captive gasket
7. composite center electrode, Ni/Cu
8. ground electrode

![The electrode end of a spark plug: center electrode and ground electrodes.](/pptx/slide48-spark-plug-electrodes.png)

</div>
</div>
</div>

<!--
The gap is a compromise. A wide gap gives a long spark that touches more mixture, so ignition is
surer. But a wider gap needs a higher voltage to break down, and if the coil cannot supply it, the
plug misfires. As the electrodes wear, the gap grows, which is why plugs are replaced.

The plug lives at up to 30 kilovolts and around 1000 degrees, and the ceramic insulator must hold
that for tens of thousands of kilometers.
-->

## Prototype: corona spark plug

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.6fr_1fr] gap-8 items-center">
<div class="text-sm">

**System operation**
- an igniter assembly, mounted in place of the spark plug, with an inductor at the top of the
  assembly and a firing tip protruding into the combustion chamber
- an AC voltage at **1 MHz** is fed to the igniter, which causes a strong electrical field at the
  firing tip that extends into the gas in the combustion chamber
- the fuel–air mix becomes ionized and forms a plasma
- with sufficient electron density, long streams of ionized gas extend further into the combustion
  chamber and ignite the charge

**Key benefits**
- more powerful ignition source
- improved lean and stratified ignition ability
- lambda ratios of up to 2.0
- higher EGR tolerance (~35 %)
- higher mean effective pressures possible
- faster burn rates and more consistent combustion

</div>
<div>

![A corona igniter firing: several long violet streamers of ionized gas branching from the tip.](/pptx/slide49-corona-igniter.png)

</div>
</div>

<!--
Instead of one spark between two electrodes, a corona igniter makes several long streamers of
plasma at once. The mixture is lit in several places, so it burns faster and very lean mixtures,
up to lambda 2, still light reliably. That matters for the lean and stratified modes of direct
injection from section 05.

Still a prototype technology; you will not find it in the hands-on engine.
-->

## Ignition system: timing for torque

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.5fr_1fr] gap-6">
<div>

- Deliver **maximum engine torque**: maximum combustion pressure should occur shortly after TDC,
  about 15° after
  - there is a delay between the moment of ignition and complete combustion, so ignition must
    occur before
  - ignition must be advanced more
    - as the engine speed increases and the charge decreases
    - for a lean A/F mixture (the flame propagates slowly)
- The advance ignition timing (AIT) is determined on the engine test bench and stored in
  **program maps**.
- Other effects influence the ignition timing
  - knock sensors

</div>

<!--
The top figure is the key one. Three sparks, three pressure traces, and the motored cylinder with no
fuel for reference. MBT, the dark blue line: the pressure peaks about 15 degrees after top dead
center, when the piston can turn it into work. Too early, 15 degrees more advance: the pressure
rises while the piston is still coming up, so it pushes against the piston; the peak is 69 bar
instead of 49. In a real engine that peak is ragged: knock, the end gas exploding instead of
burning. Too late, 15 degrees less: the piston is already moving away, and the pressure peaks at
31 bar near 30 degrees. Each costs about 7 percent of the work.

Between too early and too late there is a spark angle that gives the most torque. It is called
MBT, minimum advance for best torque. The map below holds it for every speed and manifold
pressure: about 10 degrees at idle, rising with speed, falling with load, about 35 degrees at high
speed and light load. A production map holds a knock-limited angle short of MBT at high load and
low speed, which gives the scanned map its ridges. The knock sensor pulls the spark back when it
hears detonation.

Both plots are idealized, drawn from textbook trends. The pressure comes from a few-line cylinder:
one zone, compression ratio 10.5 as in the Otto loop, a Wiebe burn of 50 degrees and an assumed
wall heat loss, at 3000 rpm and half load. The map is an assumed function with the anchors of

<!--
Three pollutants, one horizontal axis, lambda, and one curve per spark advance. The slider under

## Functions of the ignition system

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1fr_1.3fr] gap-6">
<div>

- Determine the **timing of ignition**
- Determine the **dwell period** (the coil's charging time)
  - the ignition energy depends on the primary current at the moment the current is cut off
- **Ignition release**
  - ensures that the spark occurs in the right cylinder, at the right time, with the required
    energy

</div>
<div>

## Ignition coil

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.3fr_1fr] gap-8">
<div>

- The coil is a **high-voltage transformer** made up of two coils of wire.
- The ignition coil functions as
  - an **energy-storage device**: energy is stored in the magnetic field of the core
  - a **transformer**
- The **primary** winding has far fewer turns and is powered from the low-voltage 12 V battery.
- The **secondary** consists of thousands of turns of smaller wire.

$$
\frac{U_2}{U_1} \approx \frac{N_2}{N_1}, \qquad E = \tfrac{1}{2}\,L_1 I_1^2
$$

</div>
<div>

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1fr_1.4fr] gap-2 stack items-center">

![A pencil coil for one cylinder, in section.](/pptx/slide53-coil-section.png)

![A classic canister ignition coil in cutaway: primary and secondary windings around an iron core.](/pptx/slide53-coil-cutaway.gif)

</div>

<div class="diagram-short">

![Coil ignition circuit: the battery feeds the primary winding L₁ through the ignition switch and a ballast resistor R_v; a transistor switched by the ECU's dwell pulse grounds the primary, current i₁; the secondary winding L₂, about 80 times the turns on the same core, sends up to 30 kV to the spark plug.](/diagrams/06-coil-ignition-circuit.svg)

</div>

</div>
</div>

<!--
If you think the coil looks like an electromagnet, you are right, but it is also an inductor. The
key to its operation is what happens when the primary circuit is suddenly broken, by the contact
points in an old system or a transistor in a new one. The magnetic field of the primary collapses
rapidly. The secondary is engulfed by a powerful, changing field, and that induces a very high
voltage in it, up to 100,000 volts in the extreme, because of the number of turns in the secondary.
The secondary feeds this voltage to the distributor through a very well insulated high-voltage
wire.

The two formulas: the voltage scales with the turns ratio, and the stored energy is half L I
squared in the primary. That is why the current at cut-off, and so the dwell, sets the spark energy.

The circuit is redrawn as Dyad task 9's ignition coil will model it: battery, switch, ballast
resistor, primary, a transistor switched by the ECU's dwell pulse, the secondary and one plug. The
turns ratio of 1 to 80 is task 9's assumed value. The source deck's version had contact breaker
and distributor; that circuit is on the contact-breaker slide that follows.

Notebook 08 will model this as two coupled inductors and show the secondary voltage at cut-off.
-->

## Contact-breaker ignition

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.2fr_1fr] gap-8">
<div>

- The **ignition distributor**, since replaced by the electronic control system
  - **Centrifugal advance mechanism**: adjusts the ignition timing in response to changes in
    engine speed.
  - **Vacuum advance mechanism**: adapts the ignition timing to changes in engine output and load
    factor.
  - **Ignition distributor**: routes the high voltage from the ignition coil to the spark plugs in
    the correct firing order.

![A distributor with its cap, high-tension leads, vacuum advance unit and drive shaft.](/pptx/slide54-distributor.jpeg)

</div>
<div class="stack">

![Vacuum advance mechanism: a diaphragm unit connected to the intake manifold behind the throttle pulls the breaker plate round, advancing the timing as the vacuum rises.](/pptx/slide54-advance-mechanism.png)

![Contact-breaker ignition circuit: the battery feeds the coil primary L₁ through the ignition switch; the contact breaker, opened by a cam on the distributor shaft and bridged by a capacitor, grounds the primary; the secondary L₂ sends high voltage to the distributor rotor, which passes it to the four spark plugs in turn.](/diagrams/06-contact-breaker-ignition.svg)

</div>
</div>

<!--
Before electronics, the ignition map was mechanical. Flyweights in the distributor swung out with
speed and turned the cam: that is the centrifugal advance, the speed axis of the map. A diaphragm
connected to the manifold pulled the breaker plate round: the vacuum advance, the load axis. The
rotor then sent the spark to each plug in turn.

Two axes, speed and load: the same two axes as the electronic map two slides back, implemented with
springs and weights.

The circuit at the bottom right is a schematic redrawn from the pictorial drawing the source deck
used. When the cam opens the breaker, the primary current collapses and the secondary induces the
spark; the capacitor stops the breaker from arcing. The cam and the rotor are on one shaft, so the
plug the rotor points at fires.
-->

## Types of ignition system

<div class="grid grid-cols-[1.4fr_1fr] gap-8 items-center">
<div>

- **Distributorless ignition systems**
  - one coil fires one pair of companion cylinders simultaneously
- **Coil-over-plug system**
  - each cylinder has an ignition coil mounted directly above the spark plug on the cylinder head
    cover; a short suppressor/connector connects the coil to the plug
- **Coil-near-plug system**
  - an ignition coil/module is mounted near each cylinder, with a short secondary ignition cable to
    the spark plug; each coil/module has its own control circuit and is activated sequentially by
    the PCM

</div>
<div>

![Coil-near-plug layout on a V engine: engine control module, ignition control module, one coil per cylinder with short leads to the spark plugs, crankshaft and camshaft position sensors.](/pptx/slide55-coil-near-plug.jpg)

</div>
</div>

<!--
Once the ECU knows the crank angle, the distributor is unnecessary. Distributorless systems use one
coil for each pair of cylinders that reach top dead center together; one of the pair fires on its
compression stroke, the other wastes a spark on its exhaust stroke. That is why it is also called a
wasted-spark system.

Coil-on-plug puts a coil on every plug, so there are no high-voltage leads at all, and each cylinder
can have its own timing and dwell. That is the standard today. PCM is powertrain control module, the
ECU that runs both engine and gearbox.
-->

## Summary of section 06

- The spark fires before TDC so that peak pressure lands shortly after it; the advance grows with
  speed and falls with load.
- Too early: high pressure and knock. Too late: lost torque. In between: maximum torque, MBT.
- Advance trades torque and fuel against NOx and HC.
- The coil stores energy in its primary and transforms it to tens of kilovolts; the dwell sets the
  energy.
- Distributor and breaker points gave way to distributorless and coil-on-plug ignition.
